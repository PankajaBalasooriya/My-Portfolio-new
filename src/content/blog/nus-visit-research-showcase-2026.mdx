On 13 October the Department of Computer Science and Engineering ran a research
showcase in the GTN Lab for a visiting group from the National University of
Singapore. Three hours, a room of digital posters, and a steady stream of
people walking up to ask what you are doing. Our project — cooperative
multi-drone payload transport with learning-based distributed control — had a
table near the window.

This is what I took away from it, which is mostly not about the drones.

## The event

The showcase sits on top of an agreement NUS signed with Sri Lanka's Ministry
of Education in January 2025, driven by Assoc Prof Suranga Nanayakkara. A
delegation from NUS Computing first came through Sri Lanka at the end of
October 2025 to meet prospective PhD students and talk to university
leadership, and the Department has hosted student work for visiting NUS
academics since. This year's version was ours.

The format was deliberately low-ceremony: posters shown on screens rather than
printed, a table each, and no scheduled talks. You stand next to your work and
people come to you. Setup was from 8:30, doors at 9:00, and by about 9:20 the
room was loud.

## The project, in the two minutes you actually get

One drone cannot lift a heavy or awkward load. Three can, if they cooperate.
The hard part is not the lifting — it is controlling the load's full 6-DoF
pose, position and orientation together, so a steel beam arrives level and
where you meant it rather than swinging on its cables.

Our approach is decentralised. Each drone runs its own policy and makes its own
decisions; there is no central controller holding the formation together and no
drone-to-drone communication. The payload broadcasts its own position and
orientation, and each drone reacts to that. That is the only shared signal.
The policies are trained cooperatively with multi-agent reinforcement learning
in simulation, then transferred to hardware.

The results panel shows payload position and orientation error converging

the RAPTOR controller. That last image did more work than everything else on
the poster. People believe a trajectory they can see.

## What the questions were actually about

I had prepared for questions about the learning setup — reward shaping, the
sim-to-real gap, why multi-agent rather than a single policy over a joint
action space. A few of those came. Most did not.

What people asked instead:

- **Why no communication between the drones?** The most common question by
  some distance, and the one I had the least rehearsed answer to on the poster.
  The honest version is that communication is a failure mode: links drop,
  latency varies, and a policy that depends on a message it did not receive
  behaves badly. Implicit coordination through the shared payload state is
  weaker but does not have that cliff.
- **What happens when one drone fails?** Fair, and we do not have a clean
  answer yet.
- **Does it work outside?** Yes — the figure-eight is a real flight. This
  usually ended the technical part of the conversation, which told me something
  about which evidence counts.
- **What is it for?** Construction, disaster response, moving things to places
  without roads. Asked almost every time, and I never had a short answer I was
  happy with.

## The thing I would do differently

The poster is written for someone who already knows what a quadcopter policy
is. It is not a bad poster — the brief asked for something legible to technical
and non-technical audiences, and the problem panel does that much. But the
middle of it assumes the reader will give you four minutes, and in a room like
this nobody gives you four minutes until you have earned them in forty seconds.

What earned them, every time, was the physical drone on the table. People
stopped for the hardware and stayed for the poster, never the other way around.
If I run a table like this again, the demo goes in front and the poster becomes
the thing I point at once someone is already asking.

The second thing: I explained the system three different ways over three hours
and the last version was much better than the first. Standing in a room
repeating yourself to people who do not share your assumptions is a fast way to
find out which parts of your explanation were load-bearing and which were
habit. That is worth more than the feedback itself.
